1. You manage a small retail store currently holding exactly $40,000 in liquid cash reserves. Your monthly inventory cost is $10,000. A key supplier just offered a 15% discount if you commit to a six-month upfront order, reducing the total cost from $60,000 to $51,000. Additional off-site storage will cost $500 per month. While our sales have been stable, a major new competitor is confirmed to open just two blocks away in exactly four months. Local industry data shows this typically causes an immediate 20% drop in foot traffic for similar stores. Should we take the bulk discount to save $9,000, or stick to our flexible monthly ordering?
2. You advise a small accounting firm currently spending $4,000 monthly on a part-time bookkeeper (20 hours/week at $50/hour). A new AI automation software costs $12,000 upfront plus $200/month, promising to reduce bookkeeping processing time by 80%. However, the firm’s client base is growing rapidly at a steady 15% per quarter, meaning volume will compound quickly. The software requires a strict two-year contract with a $6,000 early cancellation fee. The firm’s current cash flow is tight, with only $15,000 in the main operating account. Should we invest in the AI software to drastically cut monthly labor costs, or maintain our current staffing model and scale manually?
3. You manage a local bakery currently spending $2,000 monthly on local Facebook ads, reliably generating $8,000 in attributable revenue (a solid 4x ROAS). A marketing agency proposes a $15,000 upfront campaign to launch on a new regional delivery app, promising a 5x ROAS ($75,000 in new revenue). However, your bakery’s production capacity is strictly maxed out at $25,000 in total monthly revenue. Hiring and training a new baker to expand capacity costs $4,000 monthly and requires a firm two-month lead time. The bakery has $20,000 in total savings. Should we approve the $15,000 regional campaign, or reject it and scale local ads incrementally?
