## Core judgment
Automate, but don't pay the $12,000 upfront out of a $15,000 operating account. Neither option in the question is the best one. The better path is to adopt the software with the upfront cost financed or spread out, or with a pilot/exit clause written into the contract.

**Why the software wins:**
- **Volume growth.** 15% per quarter compounds to 1.15^8 ≈ 3.06x over the two-year term.
- **Manual staffing cost.** If you scale manually, bookkeeping goes from 20 to about 35 hours a week by quarter 4 and about 61 hours by quarter 8. Over 24 months that costs about $164,700 ($12,000 per quarter × the sum of 1.15^0 through 1.15^7, which is about 13.73).
- **Manual hits a hiring wall.** The part-time role passes 40 hours a week around quarter 5 (20 × 1.15^5 ≈ 40). After that you are recruiting and training a second person, not just adding hours.
- **Software cost.** The software path costs $16,800 over the term ($12,000 + $200 × 24). With an 80% cut, labor is about $32,900, so the total is about $49,700.
- **Savings.** That saves roughly $115,000 over two years.
- **Break-even.** The software pays for itself over the term if it cuts hours by only about 10% ($16,800 ÷ $164,700). The economics hold even if the vendor's 80% is badly oversold.
- **Payback.** At current volume the net saving is $3,000 a month ($4,000 × 0.8 − $200). Cumulative savings pass the $12,000 outlay in month 4.

**Why the cash plan fails as stated:**
- **Thin buffer.** Paying $12,000 upfront leaves $3,000. That is less than one month of the bookkeeper's cost.
- **Wrong cost base.** That cost is really about $4,333 a month ($50 × 20 × 52 ÷ 12), not $4,000, because the question assumes a 4-week month.
- **Payroll risk.** One late client payment during months 1–4 and you can't make payroll.
- **Worst case.** If the tool fails early, you have $12,000 sunk, plus monthly fees, plus a $6,000 cancellation fee. That is about $18,200, more than all the cash you have.

**What to do instead:**
- **Restructure the purchase.** Ask the vendor to spread the $12,000 over 6–12 months, or to give a 60–90 day pilot with an exit that waives the fee if the promised time savings don't show up. If they refuse, finance the $12,000 with a line of credit. The roughly $3,000 a month saving covers the repayments.
- **Keep a cash floor.** Keep at least 1–2 months of fixed costs in the account.

## Top risks
1. **Cash runs short.** Paying $12,000 from a $15,000 account leaves $3,000, below one month of payroll. One late client payment means missed payroll.
2. **Savings never reach cash.** If the bookkeeper stays at 20 hours a week, the year-1 cash saving is zero. The benefit is only avoided future hires, and the $12,000 is a pure cash drain.
3. **Contract lock-in.** If the tool underdelivers, getting out costs $6,000, due exactly when cash is already tight. Whether remaining monthly fees are also owed on exit is not stated in the question (unverified).
4. **The 80% claim may not hold for your work.** It is a vendor promise, and messy client data or exceptions still need manual work. Because break-even is about 10%, this mostly shrinks the savings rather than flipping the decision.
5. **Manual scaling is not the safe default.** About 40 hours a week by quarter 5 means a second hire, with recruiting time, training and quality risk, just as volume peaks.

## Recommended change
First step, this week: ask the vendor to split the $12,000 into installments and add a 60–90 day pilot exit that waives the $6,000 fee, and ask for 2–3 reference firms of similar size. This costs a few hours, nothing in cash. In parallel, agree with the bookkeeper whether her freed hours come off payroll or go to billable work.

I would change my mind if the reference firms report real hour reductions under about 20%, or if the vendor refuses all payment flexibility and no credit line is available. In that case, keep manual staffing for 1–2 quarters while you build cash to at least $25,000, then buy.

## Confidence
Medium. The economics are clear, but I'd want one thing confirmed first: that the upfront payment can be financed or spread, so the cash floor never drops below about one month of operating costs.

## Weakest assumption
That the hours the software frees actually come off payroll (or turn into billable work) within the first few months. If the bookkeeper's paid hours stay at 20 a week, the $12,000 is a near-term cash loss and the runway case falls apart.
