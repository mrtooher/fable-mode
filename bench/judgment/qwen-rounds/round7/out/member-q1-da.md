## Core judgment
Do not take the deal as offered. The panel will probably agree, but likely for the wrong reason. Most members will rest their case on the competitor: a 20% traffic drop in month 4 leaves you overstocked. That premise is weaker than it looks. A 20% drop in foot traffic is a typical figure for similar stores, not a forecast for this one, and it does not translate one-for-one into a 20% drop in sales. If the stock is non-perishable, any excess in months 5-6 carries over into month 7 rather than being lost. Ordering $10,000 against roughly $8,000 of demand in months 5 and 6 leaves about $4,000 of extra stock at cost. That is less than the discount saves.

The point that actually decides it is cash, and it rules out the deal on its own. $51,000 due upfront is more than the $40,000 you hold, so you would have to borrow $11,000 and empty your reserves just before a known drop in revenue. The $9,000 saving is also overstated: storage at $500 a month for six months is $3,000, so the net saving is about $6,000, before any borrowing cost.

Rejecting the deal is not the same as sticking with monthly ordering unchanged, and that is the option the question leaves out. Counter-offer the supplier with one of these:
- **(a) Commit to the volume, keep monthly delivery and payment.** Agree to six months of volume, but take delivery and pay each month at the discounted price. This gets the discount with no cash outlay and no storage cost.
- **(b) Shorten the commitment to three months.** Three months at 15% off is $25,500, saving $4,500. It leaves $14,500 in cash and ends before the competitor opens.

Monthly ordering at full price should be the fallback, not the answer.

## Top risks
- The cash shortfall ($51,000 owed against $40,000 held) forces debt or a zero balance just as revenue is about to fall.
- The real saving is about $6,000, not $9,000, once $3,000 of storage is counted, and it shrinks further with any interest cost.
- Months 5-6 of stock arrive and are paid for after traffic has dropped. That matters a great deal if the goods are perishable, seasonal or go out of date, and very little if they keep.
- Walking away without a counter-offer gives up a supplier who is clearly willing to discount.
- Treating "20% drop" as certain could lead you to under-order and run short of stock if the actual hit is smaller.

## Recommended change
This week, put option (a) to the supplier in writing: 15% off a six-month volume commitment, delivered and paid monthly. If they refuse, offer option (b). It costs one phone call and an email. I would back the original upfront deal only if the supplier offers 60 days or more to pay with no interest **and** the goods are non-perishable, because that removes the cash problem.

## Confidence
High — the deal fails on arithmetic using only the numbers in the question, whatever the competitor does. Whether the supplier will accept a counter-offer is uncertain (unverified).

## Weakest assumption
That the $40,000 is the only cash available to pay the supplier, meaning no monthly sales income or supplier payment terms arrive in time to cover the $11,000 gap. The question gives no revenue figures, so this cannot be checked.
