## Your call and this memo

Your call: "Stick to flexible monthly ordering to preserve the $40,000 cash reserve and avoid dead stock when foot traffic drops."

**Where the memo agrees:** turn down the six-month prepaid order. Both of your reasons hold against it. It costs $51,000 against your $40,000, an $11,000 gap, and if sales in months 5–6 fall in line with the 20% traffic drop it leaves about $4,000 of surplus stock. If the supplier turns down every counter-offer, the memo ends exactly where you do: plain monthly ordering, with the $40,000 kept for when the competitor opens.

**Where it differs:** the memo treats full-price monthly ordering as the fallback, not the first move. The strongest point against your call is that neither of your reasons applies to the competitor-free months. A four-month commitment covering months 1–4, delivered and invoiced monthly, costs $8,500 a month instead of $10,000. It needs no upfront payment, no off-site storage, and it ends before the competitor opens, so it adds no dead-stock risk from the traffic drop. At 15% that's $6,000 saved, and $4,000 even at 10%. That matches the prepaid deal's best case. Ordering at full price from the start gives that up, at $1,500 a month.

**What would show you're right:** the supplier refuses every monthly-payment version; or your goods don't keep and the supplier won't discount anything shorter than six months; or your cash check shows that even $8,500 a month leaves you short of two months of fixed costs. The first comes from the supplier's written reply, the third from your rent, payroll and recent receipts.

**The trade-off only you can weigh:** a four-month commitment still locks in your volume and product mix for months 1–4. Whether keeping that freedom, and skipping a negotiation, is worth $4,000–$6,000 is your call.

Your second call: keep yours, switch, or change it, and the reason in a line.
