## QUESTION 1

You manage a small retail store currently holding exactly $40,000 in liquid cash reserves. Your monthly inventory cost is $10,000. A key supplier just offered a 15% discount if you commit to a six-month upfront order, reducing the total cost from $60,000 to $51,000. Additional off-site storage will cost $500 per month. While our sales have been stable, a major new competitor is confirmed to open just two blocks away in exactly four months. Local industry data shows this typically causes an immediate 20% drop in foot traffic for similar stores. Should we take the bulk discount to save $9,000, or stick to our flexible monthly ordering?

### Q1 MEMO (six sections, written before any call)

#### Recommendation

Don't take the six-month prepaid order as offered, and don't just go back to full-price monthly ordering either. This week, make the supplier a counter-offer: keep a volume discount, but take delivery and pay monthly. Commit to six months of volume only if your goods keep. If they don't, commit to four months, ending before the competitor opens. If the supplier refuses every version, order monthly at full price.

#### Why

- **The cash doesn't cover it.** The order costs $51,000 and you hold $40,000, a gap of $11,000. The question gives no revenue figures, so it's unclear whether sales receipts or supplier payment terms would arrive in time to cover that gap. If they don't, you'd have to borrow it, and you'd start month 1 with no reserve four months before a confirmed drop in traffic. A store facing a new competitor nearby typically needs more cash, not less, to pay for markdowns, loyalty offers and slower stock turnover.
- **The real saving is much smaller than $9,000.** Storage is $500 × 6 = $3,000, so the most the deal can save is $6,000, before any interest on the $11,000. The competitor cuts it further. Suppose sales in months 5 and 6 fall in line with the 20% traffic drop. That is an assumption, because the question gives traffic figures, not sales. You'd then need about $8,000 of stock in each of those months, and six months of monthly ordering would cost $56,000. The bulk route costs $54,000 in cash and leaves about $4,000 (list value) of surplus stock. The true saving is therefore $2,000 if the surplus is written off, or $6,000 if it later sells at full price, minus storage for any month it sits past month 6. If sales don't fall, the saving is $6,000. Either way you get $2,000–$6,000 before interest, in exchange for putting about 128% of your cash into stock.
- **The problem is paying upfront, not the discount.** The same 15% on terms you can afford is worth as much or more:
  - **Four months (months 1–4), paid and delivered monthly:** $40,000 at list becomes $34,000, a saving of $6,000. That matches the prepaid deal's best case, without the cash gap or the off-site storage. Even 10% off ($4,000) beats the prepaid deal's low end of $2,000.
  - **Six months of volume, paid and delivered monthly:** $51,000, a saving of $9,000 on paper. If sales in months 5–6 fall 20%, you get the same $4,000 of surplus stock. Against $56,000 of monthly ordering, that saves $5,000 if the surplus is written off and $9,000 if it later sells at full price. It beats the four-month version when the goods keep or the sales drop is smaller. It loses by $1,000 when the surplus has to be written off.
  - It's not known whether the supplier will accept any of these terms (unverified; ask the supplier).
- **Full-price monthly ordering is the fallback, not the goal.** It keeps your cash but gives up a discount the supplier has shown it's willing to give.

#### What to do

1. **Today:** decide whether your stock keeps, meaning it's non-perishable, not seasonal and not likely to go out of date for a few months past month 6. That decides which offer you open with.
2. **This week:** send the supplier the counter-offer in writing.
   - **If the goods keep:** ask for 15% off six months of volume, delivered and invoiced monthly ($8,500 a month). If they offer less than 15% on six months, switch to the four-month offer below.
   - **If they don't keep:** ask for 15% off a four-month commitment covering months 1–4, delivered and invoiced monthly ($8,500 a month, $34,000 in total). You can accept as little as 10% ($9,000 a month, $36,000 in total).
3. **If the supplier will only discount a prepaid order:** offer at most a three-month prepaid order at 15% ($25,500, saving $4,500), with the supplier holding the stock and shipping it monthly. That leaves you $14,500 of your $40,000. If you'd have to store it off-site, three months of storage ($1,500) cuts the saving to $3,000.
4. **Whichever version you sign:** make sure you still have at least two months of fixed costs (rent, payroll) in cash after its first payment. If you don't, don't sign it.
5. **Deadline:** agree terms before your next monthly order is due. If nothing is agreed by then, place that order at full price as usual and keep negotiating. Each month at full price gives up $1,500 of discount.
6. **When to stop:** if the supplier turns down every version, go back to plain monthly ordering. Keep the $40,000 for dealing with the competitor when it opens.

#### Risks of this plan

- **The supplier may refuse every counter-offer and withdraw the deal.** You then give up the prepaid deal's $2,000–$6,000 saving (before interest). That is the price of keeping your cash.
- **The competitor may hurt less, or more slowly, than the typical 20%.** Then this plan looks worse in hindsight. The prepaid deal would have saved the full $6,000 net. And if sales don't fall, the four-month version gives up $3,000 of discount on months 5–6 compared with the six-month version.
- **The competitor may hurt more, or for longer, than the typical figure.** Then any six-month volume commitment leaves more than $4,000 of surplus. That's why the six-month offer is only for goods that keep.
- **Either commitment is binding.** It limits how far you can change your product mix after the competitor opens.
- **Waiting costs money.** Each month without agreed terms gives up $1,500 of discount.
- **The plan assumes the $40,000 isn't already needed elsewhere.** The question gives no figures for rent, payroll or margins.
- **How reversible this is:** turning down the prepaid order can be undone only while the supplier keeps the offer open. If it lapses, you lose at most $6,000 before interest. The part you can't undo is whatever you sign: four or six months of committed volume. The prepaid order would have been the hardest to undo ($51,000 paid and stock in storage), which is why the plan leaves it out. If the counter-position below turns out to be right, ask whether the original offer still stands with 60 days to pay. Take it if your cash forecast covers the $11,000 gap with that two-month buffer still in hand.

#### Strongest counter-position

**The case for taking the six-month offer:** the cash gap and the overstock are both smaller than they look. The $40,000 is a reserve, not all the cash you'll have. A store that buys $10,000 of inventory a month takes in sales receipts every month. If the supplier allows 60 days or more to pay with no interest, receipts from the first two months may close the $11,000 gap. The overstock worry rests on a typical 20% traffic drop for similar stores, which is not a forecast for this store. Traffic does not translate one-for-one into sales, and surplus stock that keeps simply carries over into month 7. On those facts the deal saves about $6,000. Meanwhile, a counter-offer risks a "no" from a supplier that has already shown it will discount.

It wins when all of the following hold:
- your goods keep;
- the supplier refuses every monthly-payment version but gives 60 or more interest-free days to pay;
- your cash forecast shows receipts covering the $11,000 with at least two months of fixed costs still in hand.

The evidence that would show it is right: the supplier's written payment terms, plus your sales receipts for the last few months.

#### Confidence

- **High:** the prepaid order costs $11,000 more than the cash you hold, and storage cuts the headline saving to $6,000 at most. This is arithmetic from the question.
- **Medium:** that the gap actually forces borrowing. This depends on payment being due when you sign and on no receipts arriving in time.
- **Medium:** the $2,000–$6,000 real saving. This rests on sales falling in line with the 20% traffic drop and on whether the surplus sells.
- **Medium:** that the monthly-paid four- or six-month versions beat the prepaid deal. The arithmetic holds if the supplier agrees.
- **Low:** that the supplier will agree.
- **Material unknowns:** when payment is due; your monthly sales receipts, margins and fixed costs; whether the goods keep; the interest cost on $11,000; how closely sales follow traffic; and how flexible the supplier is.

## QUESTION 2

You advise a small accounting firm currently spending $4,000 monthly on a part-time bookkeeper (20 hours/week at $50/hour). A new AI automation software costs $12,000 upfront plus $200/month, promising to reduce bookkeeping processing time by 80%. However, the firm’s client base is growing rapidly at a steady 15% per quarter, meaning volume will compound quickly. The software requires a strict two-year contract with a $6,000 early cancellation fee. The firm’s current cash flow is tight, with only $15,000 in the main operating account. Should we invest in the AI software to drastically cut monthly labor costs, or maintain our current staffing model and scale manually?

### Q2 MEMO (six sections, written before any call)

#### Recommendation

Automate, but don't sign this contract as written. Don't pay the $12,000 upfront out of a $15,000 operating account either. Buy only once the upfront cost is spread out or financed and you have a 60–90 day exit, and treat the payoff as hires you avoid rather than an immediate pay cut for your bookkeeper. Neither option as framed is right. Staying manual costs far more over two years, and the lump-sum purchase puts payroll at risk.

#### Why

**The economics favor automation even if the vendor's claim is badly oversold.**
- Volume growing 15% a quarter reaches 1.15^8 ≈ 3.06x over the two-year term.
- Scaling manually from today's $12,000 a quarter costs about $164,700 over 24 months ($12,000 × the sum of 1.15^0 through 1.15^7, which is about 13.73).
- Bookkeeping hours go from 20 a week to about 35 after four quarters of growth and about 40 after five (around month 15). That is past a part-time role, so you'd be hiring a second person. By month 24 it reaches about 61 hours.
- The software costs $16,800 over the term ($12,000 + 24 × $200). It breaks even if it saves about 10% of hours under the growth case ($16,800 ÷ $164,700), or about 17.5% if volume stays flat ($16,800 ÷ $96,000).
- The two-year saving depends on how it's taken:
  - About $115,000 if the full 80% holds and hours come off payroll as it does. That is $164,700 minus about $32,900 of labor and the $16,800 software cost.
  - About $51,900 if the 80% holds but you keep the bookkeeper at 20 hours a week and let the tool absorb the growth. That is $164,700 minus $96,000 of labor and $16,800 of software.
  - About $32,600 if real savings are only 30%. That is $164,700 minus 70% of the labor, about $115,300, and the $16,800 of software.
- Small firms typically see real savings of 20–50%, not 80%, after a ramp of 2–3 months (unverified; check with the vendor's reference customers). Exceptions, messy client books and review work don't shrink the way routine entries do. That shrinks the gain but doesn't reverse it.

**The danger is cash, not return.**
- Paying $12,000 from $15,000 leaves $3,000. That is less than one month of the bookkeeper alone, before rent or any other bill.
- The question's $4,000 a month assumes 4-week months. The real average is about $4,333 ($50 × 20 × 52 ÷ 12), so the gap is slightly worse than it looks.
- Payback isn't quick enough to cover the gap. At current volume, the net saving is $3,000 a month ($4,000 × 0.8 − $200), so the $12,000 is recovered in month 4. That only happens if the full 80% shows up at once and hours actually come off payroll.
- If you keep the bookkeeper's hours flat, the first year is roughly a wash: about $9,500 of avoided growth costs against the $12,000 outlay. The gain arrives in year 2.
- One late client payment in that window and you can't make payroll.

**The contract puts the risk on you.**
- If the tool disappoints, getting out costs the $12,000 already paid, plus the $6,000 fee, plus the monthly fees paid so far. That is $18,600 if you exit after three months, more than everything in the account.
- The question doesn't say whether the remaining monthly fees are also owed on exit.

**Keep the bookkeeper.**
- At 80%, her 20 hours cover about 5x current volume, more than the 3.06x growth.
- She's the person who sets the tool up and checks its output, and an accounting firm carries the liability for unchecked books.
- Cutting her to about 4 hours a week will likely lose her at the moment you need her most.

#### What to do

1. **This week:** ask the vendor for three things:
   - the $12,000 split into installments over 6–12 months;
   - a 60–90 day pilot with an exit that waives the $6,000 fee if the time savings don't show up;
   - 2–3 reference firms of your size.

   At the same time, apply for a business line of credit. Decide which fallback you'll use before the vendor answers.
2. **Set the cash gate:** don't let the operating account fall below two months of the firm's total operating costs after any upfront payment. Count cash plus committed credit. Work out that number from your books now. The question gives only the bookkeeper's cost.
3. **During the pilot:** run the tool on 3–5 real client files for 2–4 weeks alongside the current process. Your own staff time the work. Don't use the vendor's numbers.
4. **Check how you bill bookkeeping.** If you bill clients hourly, move bookkeeping clients to fixed fees before hours drop. Otherwise the savings pass to clients, not the firm.
5. **Agree with the bookkeeper up front** that freed hours go to review and billable work. Reduce her hours only gradually, and only once measured savings and growth make the spare capacity real.
6. **Decide at the end of the pilot:**
   - **20% or more measured savings, with terms that pass the cash gate:** sign.
   - **Under 20%:** pass on this vendor. The margin over the $16,800 cost is too thin to justify $18,000+ of exposure.
7. **If the vendor refuses every concession and no credit line comes through:**
   - First, price a month-to-month automation tool.
   - Failing that, stay manual for up to two quarters while cash builds to the $12,000 plus the two-month floor, then buy.
   - Don't let this run past month 12. Hours approach 40 a week around month 15, and hiring a second bookkeeper locks in the more expensive path.

#### Risks of this plan

- **Lost savings while you wait.** Negotiating, piloting, or holding out for cash pushes back savings of up to $3,000 a month at the full 80%, or about $1,000 a month at 30% ($4,000 × 0.3 − $200). Meanwhile volume rises about 15% a quarter, so every quarter of delay gets more expensive.
- **The vendor may refuse.** Strict two-year contracts with $6,000 exit fees suggest a vendor that may not offer a pilot or installments. The whole fallback path then rests on credit or a month-to-month alternative.
- **Financing has a cost.** A $12,000 draw repaid over 12 months is about $1,000 a month plus interest. If you hold the bookkeeper's hours flat, year-1 avoided costs of about $9,500 fall roughly $2,500 short of it before interest. Repayments come partly from operating cash until growth makes the savings real in year 2.
- **Savings may fall short.** If real savings land near the 10–17.5% break-even, a signed contract roughly breaks even over two years and leaves you locked in.
- **The bookkeeper may leave.** If she quits during implementation, you lose the person who makes the tool work and checks it.
- **Growth may stall.** That shrinks the volume case. The flat-volume break-even of 17.5% still leaves room.

**Cost of being wrong:**
- Negotiating and piloting can be fully undone.
- Signing the two-year contract cannot be undone cheaply. Exit exposure is $18,000 or more.
- Hiring a second bookkeeper under the manual path is hard to unwind.
- If the counter-position below is right, the cost of this plan is the savings given up during the delay, at most about $3,000 a month. You recover by signing as soon as you confirm reachable cash.

#### Strongest counter-position

**Sign now, as offered.** This plan rests on the premise that $15,000 is roughly all the cash the firm can reach. If that's wrong, the cash objection disappears and the conditions only cost money:
- A firm with receivables due within weeks or an unused credit line isn't really at a $3,000 buffer.

This wins if receivables due within 30 days, plus committed credit, would keep the account above two months of total operating costs after the $12,000 payment. Your receivables aging and the bank's credit decision will show whether it does.

#### Confidence

- **High:** automation beats manual scaling over the two-year term. This is arithmetic from the question's figures, and it holds for any real savings above about 10% (growth case) or 17.5% (flat volume).
- **High:** paying $12,000 as a lump sum from a $15,000 account risks payroll. That holds as long as $15,000 is close to the cash the firm can actually reach.
- **Low:** the vendor's 80% holds on your client mix. Expect less, with a ramp.
- **Low:** the vendor will agree to installments or a pilot exit.

**Material unknowns:**
- the firm's total monthly operating costs;
- receivables timing and credit access;
- whether bookkeeping is billed hourly;
- whether remaining monthly fees are owed on early exit;
- whether the bookkeeper will stay through the transition.

## QUESTION 3

You manage a local bakery currently spending $2,000 monthly on local Facebook ads, reliably generating $8,000 in attributable revenue (a solid 4x ROAS). A marketing agency proposes a $15,000 upfront campaign to launch on a new regional delivery app, promising a 5x ROAS ($75,000 in new revenue). However, your bakery’s production capacity is strictly maxed out at $25,000 in total monthly revenue. The $15,000 campaign stays rejected either way.

#### Why

- **The campaign fails on cash before it fails on anything else.** $15,000 is 75% of your $20,000 savings and leaves $5,000. To serve any of the new demand you would also need the baker. Assuming you pay the $4,000 a month through the two-month lead time, that is $8,000 before any extra output. Campaign plus baker comes to $23,000, which is $3,000 more than you have.
- **The promised revenue doesn't fit the bakery.** At the stated premise that production is maxed out, none of the $75,000 can be baked until capacity is added. Even on the most generous reading, total revenue can't be above $25,000 and $8,000 of it is already ad-driven, so the headroom is at most $17,000 a month. $75,000 is more than four months of that. The agency gives no timeframe for it. Orders a new delivery listing can't fill turn into cancellations and poor ratings on the channel you were paying to build.
- **The 5x is a forecast and the 4x is a track record.** The 5x is the agency's promise for a channel you have never used, and the agency is paid upfront whether it delivers or not. Both figures are revenue, not profit. Delivery apps take a commission on each order, often around 15–30% (unverified — check the app's merchant terms). Ingredients and labor come out of what remains, so a 5x return on revenue can be close to break-even on margin.
- **More local ads hit the same ceiling.** At capacity, an extra Facebook dollar buys customers you have to turn away. Ad returns also typically fall as spend rises. The current 4x may already overstate what the ads add: if you would sell out anyway, some of the $8,000 credited to the ads is sales you would have made without them.
- **At the cap, the cheapest lever is price.** $25,000 is the units you can bake times your current prices. A price rise of 8–10% on the items that sell out raises the ceiling to $27,000–$27,500 at no cost in capacity, less whatever customers the higher prices lose. Whether a $4,000-a-month baker pays off depends on how much they add and on your margin. The added monthly sales times your contribution margin has to cover $4,000. For example, at a 40% margin that is $10,000 a month of added sales.

1. **This week, confirm you are actually at the cap.** Pull the last three months of sales: total monthly revenue, and how often your top items sell out before close. Turn the agency down in writing. Before you commit, check the baker break-even from Why against your real margin. If you have stopped selling out, capacity is no longer binding at these prices. Don't hire. Roll prices back on any items that stopped selling out, and go to step 5.
4. **When the new baker is producing (about two months after the hire starts):** run the delivery-app pilot at $3,000–$5,000, sized to the added capacity. Sign with the app directly where you can. That leaves savings at about $7,000–$9,000, with the baker's $4,000 a month continuing. Expand only if pilot orders, after commission, cover ingredients, labor and the baker.
