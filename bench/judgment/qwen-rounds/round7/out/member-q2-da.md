## Core judgment
The others will likely say "buy, because growth compounds the savings." On paper they are right. Manual labor over 8 quarters at 15% growth comes to about $164,700 ($12,000 per quarter × 13.73, since 1.15^8 ≈ 3.06). The software costs $16,800 over the full contract ($12,000 + 24 × $200). So it pays for itself if it saves about 10% of hours under the growth case, or about 17.5% if volume stays flat. The vendor's 80% claim barely matters for the two-year return.

The premise I attack is a different one: that an investment that pays off is one the firm can afford right now. Paying $12,000 out of $15,000 leaves $3,000. That is less than one month of this bookkeeper's pay, before rent, owner draws or any other bill. It happens just as fast growth starts eating cash: client payments come in late and you have to hire ahead of demand. Small firms that are growing fast usually fail from running out of cash, not from thin margins.

The contract terms show who carries the risk. The vendor gets $12,000 up front, keeps you for two years, and charges a $6,000 exit fee. If the product disappoints, you have spent $12,000 and still owe $6,000 to leave. A vendor confident that customers stay because the product works would not need that lock-in. So my answer is neither of the two options offered. Do not sign this contract on these terms, and do not stay manual either. **Buy automation only on terms that fit your cash**: spread the $12,000 over the contract, add a 60–90-day pilot you can exit, or have a credit line in place before signing. Or start with a month-to-month tool. Treat the payoff as hires you avoid, not as cutting your current bookkeeper. At about 3x volume you would need about 61 hours a week done by hand, so keep her. She is the person who will set the tool up and check its output, and your firm carries the liability for its errors.

## Top risks
- Liquidity: a $3,000 buffer after signing means one late client payment or a slow setup period could leave you unable to make payroll.
- Vendor's 80% figure: it probably counts only clean, routine entries. Reviewing exceptions, cleaning up messy books and talking to clients don't shrink, so the real reduction could be 30–50% (unverified). That still pays off over two years, but more slowly, while your cash is at its lowest.
- Billing model: if you bill clients hourly for bookkeeping (unverified), cutting 80% of the hours cuts that revenue too unless you switch to fixed fees. The clients keep the savings, not the firm.
- Losing the bookkeeper: dropping her to 4 hours a week means she will probably quit. That takes away the person who makes the tool work and checks its mistakes, which is a real risk for an accounting firm.
- Lock-in: the total cost of a mistake is the $12,000 already paid, plus the $6,000 exit fee, plus the months of $200 already paid. That is at least $18,000, more than you have in the bank.

## Recommended change
Before signing, ask the vendor to spread the $12,000 over the contract or add a 90-day out clause, and open a credit line in parallel. This takes about 1–2 weeks and costs nothing but negotiation time. I would switch to "sign now as offered" if the firm can show cash or committed credit covering at least 2–3 months of total operating costs after the $12,000 payment.

## Confidence
medium — automation clearly wins on unit economics; the case against signing now depends on the firm's full monthly costs and how quickly clients pay, and the question doesn't give those.

## Weakest assumption
That $15,000 is roughly all the cash the firm can reach. If it actually has receivables coming in or an unused credit line, the cash objection goes away and the plain "buy" answer is right.
