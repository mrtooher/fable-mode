## Core judgment
Don't take the bulk deal as offered. The store can't pay for it. The order is $51,000 upfront and you hold $40,000, so you are $11,000 short on day one. Even if you borrowed the gap, you'd have no cash cushion for rent, payroll or surprises. That would happen just as a competitor arrives and cuts the sales that refill your cash.

The real saving is also much smaller than $9,000:
- **Storage:** $500 × 6 months = $3,000, which brings the saving down to $6,000.
- **The competitor:** it opens after month 4, so months 5 and 6 are covered by the bulk order. If sales fall 20% in line with foot traffic, you'd need about $8,000 a month in those two months instead of $10,000. Six months of monthly ordering would then cost $56,000. The bulk route costs $54,000 in cash and leaves you about $4,000 (list value) of extra stock.
- **The result:** you save $2,000 if that extra stock has to be written off, and up to $6,000 if it sells later at full value. Storing it past month 6 costs extra. That's $2,000–$6,000 at best, for putting about 128% of your cash into stock.

The question leaves out a better choice: get most of the discount without prepaying. Offer the supplier a volume commitment with monthly delivery and monthly payment (a blanket order), or a shorter commitment that ends at month 4, before the competitor opens. Four months at 15% would save up to $6,000 ($40,000 list down to $34,000) with no storage cost and no single large payment. That matches the best case of the bulk deal without the cash or overstock risk. Even a 10% discount on those terms ($4,000) beats the likely bulk outcome. Keep the cash for responding to the competitor.

## Top risks
- Paying $51,000 against $40,000 in cash forces you to borrow at least $11,000 and leaves no buffer. One bad month could mean missed payroll or rent.
- Foot traffic drops 20% after month 4, about when your cash is lowest, so the cash that would normally refill your reserves shrinks.
- In months 5 and 6, about $4,000 (list value) of stock you can't sell keeps costing storage and may need to be marked down or written off.
- You lose flexibility: you can't change the product mix to compete with the new store because your stock is already bought.
- Once you subtract storage and overstock, the "$9,000 saving" is really $2,000–$6,000, which doesn't justify the risk.

## Recommended change
This week, call the supplier and propose a 4-month volume commitment (about $40,000 list) at 10–15% off, delivered and invoiced monthly. It costs one conversation and could save $4,000–$6,000 with no storage and no cash spike. I'd change my view only if the supplier offered the full 6-month discount with payment terms of 120 days or more, or monthly payments, and the goods would still sell after month 6.

## Confidence
High. The arithmetic is from the question: the $51,000 payment is more than the $40,000 in cash, and storage plus the competitor's effect cut the real saving to $2,000–$6,000.

## Weakest assumption
That "upfront" means you pay when you sign. If the supplier would accept payment spread across the six months, or on long terms, the cash shortfall goes away and the decision comes down to the $2,000–$6,000 saving against the overstock risk.
